# Michael Jordan — What He Actually Did (V3)

### I1 | kind=vo | section=intro

What Michael Jordan lifted, what he ate, and the six a.m. sessions nobody saw.

### H1 | kind=vo | section=hook

Start with the part that gets left out. Jordan did not win because he could jump. He could always jump. For three straight years he was the best player in the league and went home early anyway, beaten by a team that had worked out something simple: he was not strong enough to take a beating.

### H2 | kind=vo | section=hook

Detroit knocked Chicago out of the playoffs in nineteen eighty-eight, eighty-nine, and ninety. Twice in the Conference Finals. Their defensive plan had a name.

### H3 | kind=vo | section=hook

The Jordan Rules were not complicated. Every time he drove, he got hit. Hard, and by more than one man. The bet was that a two-hundred-pound guard would stop coming. For three seasons the bet paid.

### R1 | kind=vo | section=role

Michael Jeffrey Jordan was born in Brooklyn in February nineteen sixty-three and raised in Wilmington, North Carolina. The story everybody knows is that he was cut from his high school varsity team as a sophomore. The story is roughly true: he was left off the varsity roster at fifteen, at five foot ten, and spent that season on junior varsity.

### R2 | kind=vo | section=role

He grew four inches that year and spent the summer on a court in his own back garden, playing his older brother Larry, who beat him regularly. He has said since that everything competitive in him started in that garden and never stopped.

### R3 | kind=vo | section=role

North Carolina, under Dean Smith. A freshman, and the last shot of the nineteen eighty-two national championship. Chicago drafted him third in nineteen eighty-four, behind Hakeem Olajuwon and Sam Bowie.

### R4 | kind=vo | section=role

What followed was five years of individual brilliance and zero team success. Rookie of the Year. Scoring champion, year after year. Sixty-three points against Boston in the playoffs in nineteen eighty-six, still the record, in a game his team lost. In nineteen eighty-eight, the scoring title and Defensive Player of the Year in the same season, which almost nobody has ever done.

### R5 | kind=vo | section=role

And none of it survived contact with Detroit. The gap was not skill. It was what happened to his body over a seven-game series against men who had decided to make every possession expensive.

### R6 | kind=vo | section=role

He weighed about a hundred and ninety-five pounds. He was, by his own account, getting knocked to the floor and getting up slower each year. The talent was never the problem. The frame carrying it was.

### R7 | kind=vo | section=role

So in the summer of nineteen ninety he did the thing that separates him from every other gifted athlete of his era. He treated it as an engineering problem.

### T1 | kind=vo | section=turn

The man who solved it was a young trainer from Chicago named Tim Grover, with a kinesiology degree, almost no clients, and a habit of writing letters to professional athletes.

### T2 | kind=bite | quote=grover_letter

### T3 | kind=vo | section=turn

Most of those letters were never opened. This one was, because Grover had read that Jordan was tired of being knocked around, and had written to the one player in the league with a reason to answer.

### T3b | kind=vo | section=turn

Jordan agreed to thirty days. Nothing more. If it did not work, it ended.

### T3c | kind=vo | section=turn

Thirty days became fifteen years. This is how Grover describes what he started him on.

### T4 | kind=bite | quote=grover_program

### T5 | kind=vo | section=turn

The goal was never size for its own sake. It was the ability to absorb contact and keep the same body at the end of a series as at the start. By the following season he had put on about fifteen pounds of muscle.

### W1 | kind=vo | section=workout

The sessions ran before dawn, at his house north of Chicago. Scottie Pippen came. Ron Harper came. Randy Brown came. Players who had watched him lose started turning up in the dark to lift with him, and somebody gave it a name.

### W1b | kind=vo | section=workout

Grover has described how the mornings actually ran.

### W1c | kind=bite | quote=grover_breakfast

### W1d | kind=vo | section=workout

By the second three-peat this was simply how the team started its day. Players who had joined to keep up with him stayed because it worked on them too.

### W2 | kind=vo | section=workout

The program itself is unglamorous, and that is the point of it.

### W3 | kind=vo | section=workout

Squats and deadlifts for the base. Power cleans and weighted step-ups for the ability to produce force fast. Good mornings and core work for the spine that was taking the hits. Bench press and curls last, because they mattered least.

### W4 | kind=vo | section=workout

Grover has said the hardest part of the job was never motivating him. It was getting him to stop. Give him six repetitions, and he did twelve, every set, for fifteen years.

### W6 | kind=vo | section=workout

Jordan's own account of what he did, recorded at the time, is almost boringly orthodox. Which is the interesting part: there is no secret in it.

### W7 | kind=bite | quote=mj_conditioning

### W8 | kind=vo | section=workout

Note what he puts first. Not the weights.

### W9 | kind=vo | section=workout

He started with stretching, then built conditioning with repeated sprints. The weights were there to improve strength and stamina, not to replace the work on the court.

### W10 | kind=vo | section=workout

And one detail from Grover has been visible in every photograph of Jordan tired, for forty years, without anybody explaining it.

### W11 | kind=vo | section=workout

Grover told him to grab the hem of his shorts instead of leaning on his knees. It shifted some of the load away from his legs and let him recover without advertising exhaustion to an opponent.

### W12 | kind=vo | section=workout

That is the level the program was built at. Not a routine copied off a wall chart, but a specific answer to a specific body, measured and adjusted. Grover's starting point was that Jordan was not symmetrical, and that no serious program could treat him as if he were.

### W13 | kind=vo | section=workout

One side might use fifty pounds while the other used ten, or complete a different number of repetitions. The objective was not symmetry on paper. It was balance in motion.

### W14 | kind=vo | section=workout

In nineteen ninety that was not how professional basketball players were prepared. Mostly they were not prepared at all. Lifting was thought to ruin a shooting touch, and half the league still believed it.

### W14b | kind=vo | section=workout

Chip Schaefer joined the Bulls as head athletic trainer that same year, and walked into exactly that moment.

### W14d | kind=bite | quote=schaefer_1990

### W14e | kind=vo | section=workout

Nineteen ninety is when weight training in professional basketball starts being taken seriously at all. Jordan was not following that shift. He was, with a trainer nobody had heard of, several months ahead of it.

### W15 | kind=vo | section=workout

The other thing Grover describes is the one that cannot be programmed, and it is the reason the same routine does not produce the same result in anybody else.

### W16 | kind=vo | section=workout

At the highest level, the discomfort was not an accident around the training. It was part of what Jordan chose to return to every day. The exercises could be copied. That appetite could not.

### D1 | kind=vo | section=diet

Which brings us to the food, where he was genuinely unusual for his era.

### D2 | kind=vo | section=diet

The nineteen eighties and nineties told athletes one thing: carbohydrates. Rice, pasta, bread, and more of it. Jordan's plan went the other way.

### D3 | kind=vo | section=diet

A nutritionist built his plan around his body chemistry and the amount of energy he was actually spending. The result was specific, including the timing, which is the part that matters.

### D4 | kind=vo | section=diet

On game days, Jordan ate steak and a potato about four hours before tipoff. He said the meal gave him the energy to play hard and still recover. The unusual part was not the food. It was the clock.

### D5 | kind=vo | section=diet

Around that, nothing exotic. Three meals, eaten on a schedule, built around when he trained rather than when he was hungry.

### D6 | kind=bite | quote=mj_meals

### D7 | kind=vo | section=diet

Grover's view on the endless argument about which diet is correct is simpler than the marketing around it.

### D8 | kind=vo | section=diet

Every serious diet can work if the athlete actually follows it. The real question is whether it fits that body, that workload, and the time horizon long enough to matter.

### P1 | kind=vo | section=practice

There is one more component, and it is the one people copy least, because it cannot be bought or scheduled. He treated practice as the competition.

### P2 | kind=bite | quote=mj_practice

### P3 | kind=vo | section=practice

Teammates have spent thirty years describing what that was like to be on the other side of. Grover watched it from the sideline for a decade and a half, and the logic was plain.

### P4 | kind=vo | section=practice

As soon as the whistle blew, the room went quiet. Jordan's explanation was simple: practice hard enough that the game becomes easier. It also filtered out teammates who could not meet the standard.

### P5 | kind=vo | section=practice

Make the practice harder than the game, and the game becomes the easy part. It is the oldest idea in sport. He is simply the most complete example of somebody actually doing it, which is also why the training held. A man who competes that hard in an empty gym does not need supervising at six in the morning.

### S1 | kind=vo | section=results

Nineteen ninety-one. Chicago met Detroit again in the Conference Finals.

### S2 | kind=vo | section=results

Four games to nothing. The Pistons walked off the floor before the clock ran out. Chicago beat the Lakers for the first championship, and then did not stop: six titles in eight years, with two years off in the middle.

### S3 | kind=vo | section=results

The most famous night of all is the one people still get wrong. Game five of the nineteen ninety-seven Finals, Utah, series tied at two. Thirty-eight points from a man who could barely stand. It is remembered as the Flu Game. Grover was in the room.

### S4 | kind=vo | section=results

Late that night, with room service closed, one pizza was delivered to Jordan's room. He was the only person who ate it. Around three in the morning, Grover found him curled into a ball.

### S5 | kind=vo | section=results

Jordan did not publicly confirm the food-poisoning account until The Last Dance, twenty-three years later.

### S6 | kind=vo | section=results

Whatever name history gives the illness, the physical fact is clear. He was depleted, shivering, and barely able to stand before the game. Then he walked onto the floor.

### S7 | kind=vo | section=results

He played forty-four minutes.

### E1V4 | kind=vo | section=end

Strip away the mythology and the program is almost ordinary: mobility, conditioning, strength, food, sleep, repeat. The difference was not the exercise. It was the decision to return to it before dawn, every day, for fifteen years.

### E2V4 | kind=vo | section=end

Talent made Michael Jordan extraordinary. The system made that talent available in May and June — through contact, fatigue, and one more possession.
