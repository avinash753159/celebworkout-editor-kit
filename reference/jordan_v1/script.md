# Michael Jordan — What He Actually Did

**Speakers:** Michael Jordan, and Tim Grover — his personal trainer from 1989
until 2003. Grover is not a substitute for Jordan; he is the only person who can
say what was actually programmed, and why.

**A note on the shape.** Jordan is guarded, and almost none of his recorded
speech is about method — he talks about winning, opponents and pressure. What he
did say about conditioning and diet is used here in full, every second of it.
The method is carried by the man who wrote it. See `qc/BITE_REVIEW.md`.

### I1 | kind=vo | section=intro

What Michael Jordan lifted, what he ate, and the six a.m. sessions nobody saw.

### H1 | kind=vo | section=hook

Start with the part that gets left out. Jordan did not win because he could jump.
He could always jump. For three straight years he was the best player in the
league and went home early anyway, beaten by a team that had worked out
something simple: he was not strong enough to take a beating.

### H2 | kind=vo | section=hook

Detroit knocked Chicago out of the playoffs in nineteen eighty-eight,
eighty-nine and ninety. Twice in the Conference Finals. Their defensive plan
had a name.

### H3 | kind=vo | section=hook

The Jordan Rules were not complicated. Every time he drove, he got hit. Hard,
and by more than one man. The bet was that a two hundred pound guard would stop
coming. For three seasons the bet paid.

### R1 | kind=vo | section=role

Michael Jeffrey Jordan was born in Brooklyn in February nineteen sixty-three and
raised in Wilmington, North Carolina. The story everybody knows is that he was
cut from his high school varsity team as a sophomore. The story is roughly true
— he was left off the varsity roster at fifteen, at five foot ten, and spent
that season on junior varsity.

### R2 | kind=vo | section=role

He grew four inches that year and spent the summer on a court in his own back
garden, playing his older brother Larry, who beat him regularly. He has said
since that everything competitive in him started in that garden and never
stopped.

### R3 | kind=vo | section=role

North Carolina, under Dean Smith. A freshman, and the last shot of the
nineteen eighty-two national championship. Chicago drafted him third in
nineteen eighty-four, behind Hakeem Olajuwon and Sam Bowie.

### R4 | kind=vo | section=role

What followed was five years of individual brilliance and zero team success.
Rookie of the Year. Scoring champion, year after year. Sixty-three points
against Boston in the playoffs in nineteen eighty-six — still the record — in a
game his team lost. In nineteen eighty-eight, the scoring title and Defensive
Player of the Year in the same season, which almost nobody has ever done.

### R5 | kind=vo | section=role

And none of it survived contact with Detroit. The gap was not skill. It was what
happened to his body over a seven game series against men who had decided to
make every possession expensive.

### R6 | kind=vo | section=role

He weighed about a hundred and ninety-five pounds. He was, by his own account,
getting knocked to the floor and getting up slower each year. The talent was
never the problem. The frame carrying it was.

### R7 | kind=vo | section=role

So in the summer of nineteen ninety he did the thing that separates him from
every other gifted athlete of his era. He treated it as an engineering problem.

### T1 | kind=vo | section=turn

The man who solved it was a young trainer from Chicago named Tim Grover, with a
kinesiology degree, almost no clients, and a habit of writing letters to
professional athletes.

### T2 | kind=bite | quote=grover_letter

### T3 | kind=vo | section=turn

Most of those letters were never opened. This one was, because Grover had read
that Jordan was tired of being knocked around, and had written to the one player
in the league with a reason to answer.

### T3b | kind=vo | section=turn

Jordan agreed to thirty days. Nothing more. If it did not work, it ended.

### T3c | kind=vo | section=turn

Thirty days became fifteen years. This is how Grover describes what he started
him on.

### T4 | kind=bite | quote=grover_program

### T5 | kind=vo | section=turn

The goal was never size for its own sake. It was the ability to absorb contact
and keep the same body at the end of a series as at the start. By the following
season he had put on about fifteen pounds of muscle.

### W1 | kind=vo | section=workout

The sessions ran before dawn, at his house north of Chicago. Scottie Pippen
came. Ron Harper came. Randy Brown came. Players who had watched him lose
started turning up in the dark to lift with him, and somebody gave it a name.

### W1b | kind=vo | section=workout

Grover has described how the mornings actually ran.

### W1c | kind=bite | quote=grover_breakfast

### W1d | kind=vo | section=workout

By the second three-peat this was simply how the team started its day. Players
who had joined to keep up with him stayed because it worked on them too.

### W2 | kind=vo | section=workout

The programme itself is unglamorous, and that is the point of it.

### W3 | kind=vo | section=workout

Squats and deadlifts for the base. Power cleans and weighted step-ups for the
ability to produce force fast. Good mornings and core work for the spine that
was taking the hits. Bench press and curls last, because they mattered least.

### W4 | kind=vo | section=workout

Grover has said the hardest part of the job was never motivating him. It was
getting him to stop. Give him six repetitions, and he did twelve — every set,
for fifteen years.

### W6 | kind=vo | section=workout

Jordan's own account of what he did, recorded at the time, is almost boringly
orthodox. Which is the interesting part — there is no secret in it.

### W7 | kind=bite | quote=mj_conditioning

### W8 | kind=vo | section=workout

Note what he puts first. Not the weights.

### W9 | kind=bite | quote=mj_stretch_sprints

### W10 | kind=vo | section=workout

And one detail from Grover that has been visible in every photograph of Jordan
tired, for forty years, without anybody explaining it.

### W12 | kind=vo | section=workout

That is the level the programme was built at. Not a routine copied off a wall
chart — a specific answer to a specific body, measured and adjusted. Grover's
starting point was that Jordan was not symmetrical, and that no serious
programme could treat him as if he were.

### W13 | kind=bite | quote=grover_asymmetry

### W14 | kind=vo | section=workout

In nineteen ninety that was not how professional basketball players were
prepared. Mostly they were not prepared at all — lifting was thought to ruin a
shooting touch, and half the league still believed it.

### W14b | kind=vo | section=workout

Chip Schaefer joined the Bulls as head athletic trainer that same year, and
walked into exactly that moment.

### W14d | kind=bite | quote=schaefer_1990

### W14e | kind=vo | section=workout

Nineteen ninety is when weight training in professional basketball starts being
taken seriously at all. Jordan was not following that shift. He was, with a
trainer nobody had heard of, several months ahead of it.

### W15 | kind=vo | section=workout

The other thing Grover describes is the one that cannot be programmed, and it is
the reason the same routine does not produce the same result in anybody else.

### W16 | kind=bite | quote=grover_pain

### D1 | kind=vo | section=diet

Which brings us to the food, where he was genuinely unusual for his era.

### D2 | kind=vo | section=diet

The nineteen eighties and nineties told athletes one thing: carbohydrates. Rice,
pasta, bread, and more of it. Jordan's plan went the other way.

### D3 | kind=vo | section=diet

A nutritionist built his plan around his body chemistry and the amount of energy
he was actually spending, and arrived somewhere nobody else in the league was.
He described the result himself, including the timing, which is the part that
matters.

### D4 | kind=bite | quote=mj_steak

### D5 | kind=vo | section=diet

Around that, nothing exotic. Three meals, eaten on a schedule, built around when
he trained rather than when he was hungry.

### D6 | kind=bite | quote=mj_meals

### D7 | kind=vo | section=diet

Grover's view on the endless argument about which diet is the correct one is
worth hearing, because he has tested more of them on elite bodies than almost
anyone alive.

### D8 | kind=bite | quote=grover_diets

### P1 | kind=vo | section=practice

There is one more component, and it is the one people copy least, because it
cannot be bought or scheduled. He treated practice as the competition.

### P2 | kind=bite | quote=mj_practice

### P3 | kind=vo | section=practice

Teammates have spent thirty years describing what that was like to be on the
other side of. Grover watched it from the sideline for a decade and a half, and
puts the logic of it plainly.

### P4 | kind=bite | quote=grover_practice

### P5 | kind=vo | section=practice

Make the practice harder than the game, and the game becomes the easy part. It
is the oldest idea in sport. He is simply the most complete example of somebody
actually doing it — which is also why the training held. A man who competes that
hard in an empty gym does not need supervising at six in the morning.

### S1 | kind=vo | section=results

Nineteen ninety-one. Chicago met Detroit again in the Conference Finals.

### S2 | kind=vo | section=results

Four games to nothing. The Pistons walked off the floor before the clock ran
out. Chicago beat the Lakers for the first championship, and then did not stop —
six titles in eight years, with two years off in the middle.

### S3 | kind=vo | section=results

The most famous night of all is the one people still get wrong. Game five of the
nineteen ninety-seven Finals, Utah, series tied at two. Thirty-eight points from
a man who could barely stand. It is remembered as the Flu Game. Grover was in
the room.

### S5 | kind=vo | section=results

Jordan himself did not confirm any of that until The Last Dance, twenty-three
years later.

### E1 | kind=vo | section=end

The honest summary of all of it is that there is no trick. Squats, sprints,
stretching, steak, sleep, and a decision taken in one specific summer to stop
being the most talented man on the floor and become the strongest one as well.

### E2 | kind=vo | section=end

He was not born the finished product. He was the one who noticed what was
missing, and then did the boring thing about it every morning for fifteen years.
