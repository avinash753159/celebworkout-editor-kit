I. Who he is

Couldn't get into film school. Taught himself editing and storytelling while working at a content company in Montreal, learning what makes things go viral. Posted a video about Jake Paul and Dana White in late 2021, found the MMA audience, and never left.

"YouTube is the perfect playground to learn how to edit or tell the story properly." Patrick Gavia

Sixteen documentaries since. All MMA. Most between 20 and 30 minutes. Over 21 million views and close to 300,000 subscribers. He is not a studio and he is not a hobbyist — he is one obsessive with four employees, which is roughly the scale you are trying to reach.

The cold open is a fixed formula

All three films analysed in depth open identically. This is a template, not a style.

  1. Direct address to a specific artifact. "Pay attention to this little girl." · "Pay attention to the man in the background." · "It started with a video." Never a biography. Never a date.
  2. The artifact is a real recorded document — a photo-op, a Google Street View capture from twelve years earlier, a backyard fight video. Something that exists.
  3. An explicit withheld promise, stated in words, very early. In the 8.5M-view film it lands at 0:14: "I'll reveal later what she whispered to him."
  4. A rapid cascade of archival voices — several sources, minimal narration.
  5. The central question spoken aloud. "Will he get to the top or self-destruct along the way?" · "How could a backyard fighter get so big he almost destroys an entire sport?"

Bite-dominant from about 2:00

From roughly two minutes in, the transcripts are overwhelmingly first-person interview material — including the interviewer's questions, used as structure. The narrator sets up and hands off. He does not explain what you just heard.

Title grammar

The [NOUN] That/Who [VERB]ED [SUBJECT] — every title names a hidden cause and an effect on a known subject:

The Girl Who Broke Khamzat Chimaev · The Fruit that Showed Conor McGregor His OWN Death · Why the UFC Broke Kimbo Slice · The Night that Ended Islam Makhachev's Career · The UFC Champ Who FAKED His Life

VII. What to take

  • Automate the two weeks he pays humans for. Story research and video research are the whole front half of his schedule and the entire stage your pipeline lacks. That is where a machine wins outright — indexing, transcribing, searching, cutting complete thoughts.
  • Adopt the beat sheet. Two hours, scene by scene, written before the footage hunt and used to brief it. Not a finished script. The braid outline is already this shape.
  • Adopt the cold-open formula literally. Five parts, artifact first, withheld promise inside the first twenty seconds, central question spoken aloud.
  • Adopt the title grammar. Hidden cause plus known subject, with the channel's search terms carried behind it.
  • Do not copy his order blindly. He writes the beat sheet before the footage hunt because he already knows the MMA archive by heart. You don't have that per celebrity — which is exactly what an inventory pass substitutes for. Inventory → beat sheet → targeted hunt.
  • Do not automate the edit first. It is the one thing the professional refuses to hand over even to a full-time assistant. Machines can catch the mechanical failures — echo, mid-sentence cuts, over-mined sources, missing exercises. Final judgement on the cut is the last thing to give away, not the first.
The trade

Buy back the two weeks he spends on research, and spend twenty minutes on the thing he considers irreplaceable. That is a better deal than an unattended machine handing you a finished film — and it is the deal his own numbers argue for.
